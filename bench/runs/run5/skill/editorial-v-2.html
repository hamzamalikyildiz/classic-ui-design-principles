Every map is a promise about what to leave out

Paper atlases were argued over for centuries, and the arguments were rarely about accuracy. They were about who got to sit at the center of the sheet.

By Ines Halvorsen · 11 min read

A map begins as a decision about what to leave out. The cartographer who draws a coastline has to choose which of its thousands of inlets survive the reduction, and every choice is a small argument about what matters to the person who will eventually unfold the sheet on a kitchen table.

Consider the harbor in Figure 1. On the survey plan its breakwater is a jagged line, and a navigator needs every notch of it. On a coastal atlas the same breakwater shrinks to a single stroke, and the small bays beside it vanish. The shore has not changed. What changed is the reader, and what the reader was expected to do with the paper.

The problem of the straight line

Distance is the first casualty. A chart drawn at one scale tells the truth about the water between two points only so long as the scale is honest about itself. Once a sheet is folded, reprinted or projected onto a curved globe, the printed ratio becomes a rough suggestion. Sailors learned this early. They trusted the coast more than the grid, and they carried tables of their own.

The route in the figure runs through open water, and its length depends on which map you trust. A straight ruler laid across the harbor chart gives a comfortable number. Follow the arc of the ferry through the swell and the number grows, which is why the timetable and the chart rarely agree.

Who sits at the center

Every projection picks a place to be correct. Most of the famous ones put the equator or a prime meridian at the heart of the sheet, and the rest of the world bends to fit. The bending is not neutral. Countries near the edges look smaller and more distant, and generations of schoolchildren absorbed that proportion before they ever questioned it.

None of this requires bad faith. A projection is a trade, and each trade gives something up. The honest question is never whether a map distorts, because all of them do. The better question is which distortion the mapmaker chose, and whether anyone told the reader it was there.

“A map is the only document that asks you to trust its silences as much as its lines.”

Reading the silence

Practice the habit on the figure. The grid behind the coastline is not decoration; it marks where the surveyor took readings, and the places where the lines run thin are where the surveyor was least sure. A careful reader asks what was measured, what was inferred and what was simply drawn to look finished.

That habit travels. A transit map, a flood zone diagram and a political border all make the same kind of promise, and each one is easier to trust once you know what it chose to omit.
