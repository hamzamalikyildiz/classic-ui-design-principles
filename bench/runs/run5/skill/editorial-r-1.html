The Quiet Return of the Paper Map

Phones were supposed to end the folded atlas. A generation that never learned to refold one is now buying them again.

By Nadia Okafor · November 2026 · 14 min read

The map on the kitchen table was creased in eight places, and every crease had a history. One fold had soaked up tea in 1998. Another had been worn soft by a thumb tracing a ridge line on a coach journey nobody remembers. For most of the twentieth century a folded sheet was how a person found the way, and it was also a record of everywhere they had already been.

The phone took that job so completely that few people noticed it leave. Blue dots now mark where we are, and a calm voice says when to turn. Ask a teenager to open a road atlas and you may get a careful look, as if the object were a piece of equipment from a museum with no labels. Publishers of printed maps reported falling sales for almost a decade. Several closed their cartography departments and sold their archives.

Why people are unfolding again

Something shifted in the years after the pandemic. Walkers on the long-distance paths began carrying paper again, partly because batteries die on exposed ridges and partly because a sheet shows the whole country at once. A phone shows the next turn. A map shows the pass you will cross at four in the afternoon, the stream you will need to cross before it, and the farm track that cuts a corner you had not noticed.

Small publishers have noticed. Survey sheets that once sat in dusty shop drawers now sell out in a season, and a few independent cartographers have built waiting lists for hand-drawn editions. Their buyers are rarely the people who grew up with paper. They are often people who came to it late and found that the paper map asks for something the phone does not: a decision about direction, made before the first step.

A map asks you to be somewhere in your head before you are somewhere on the ground.

Tomasz Veld, surveyor and printer

Veld runs a small press in a converted mill and prints forty sheets a year, each one checked by hand against the original survey. He does not expect the phone to disappear. He expects the paper map to become a choice, the way a mechanical watch became one. The paper is slower to read, harder to carry in the rain, and impossible to update. Those qualities, he argues, are exactly what some readers want back.

There is a quiet pleasure in the scale that screens rarely give. A paper sheet covers a valley with the whole valley in view, so the eye can wander between a summit and a village without a zoom gesture. Readers describe planning a route on the kitchen floor, weights on the corners, pencil in hand. The map becomes a shared object for a household, not a private screen in a pocket.
