Every map is a small lie, told on purpose

A flat sheet cannot hold a round world. Choosing which distortion to accept decides who looks big, who looks small, and where north appears to sit.

Ines Varga / Maps and borders / 2 November 2026 11 min read

Hold a globe in one hand and a sheet of paper in the other and the problem shows up within a minute. The sphere cannot be unrolled. Every flat map has to tear, stretch or squeeze the surface somewhere, and the mapmaker's real job is to decide where the damage will sit.

The Mercator projection, published in 1569 for navigators, solved one problem beautifully. A line of constant compass bearing became a straight line, so a sailor could plot a course with a ruler. The price was area. Land near the poles swells, and generations of schoolchildren learned to picture Greenland as a rival to Africa.

What a straight line promises

Every projection makes a promise, and every promise is paid for somewhere else. Equal-area projections such as Gall–Peters keep each region in its true proportion, but they shear the shapes, so a continent can look taller than it is wide. Conformal projections keep angles and local outlines right, which suits navigation, but they inflate areas toward the edges.

“A projection is never a picture of the world. It is a decision about which things to keep wrong.”

Walk through a school atlas and you are reading a set of compromises someone has already made for you. The page carries a choice of centre, a choice of which pole sits at the top, and a choice of which oceans get cut into pieces. Nothing on the sheet says a different decision was available.

Reading the bend

The honest move is to read the margins first. Lines of latitude that curve away from the equator tell you the projection is conic or azimuthal. Grid lines that stay parallel tell you the sheet is cylindrical. When the graticule is missing altogether, the map is asking for trust it has not earned.

Small errors are rarely the danger. A map that keeps city distances within a few percent is useful for nearly everything. The trouble starts when one sheet is asked to do two jobs at once, showing the size of nations to a classroom and the bearing of ships to a harbour pilot.

The best cartographers have always said so out loud. They print the projection name in the corner, draw the scale bar in both kilometres and miles, and leave the pole visible. A small lie told in the open is a feature. A lie that hides its own seams is the one people remember.
