The Quiet Return of the Paper Map

Phones promised to make the folded sheet obsolete. Instead, a generation that never learned to read one is buying them by the armful, and finding out what a map can do that a blue dot cannot.

By Mira Halvorsen · Photographs and cartography by the author · 14 min read

Part one

Every spring, the stationery shop on Calder Street puts a rack of folded maps beside the till, and every spring someone buys one for the wall. The owner, Teodor Mink, has run the shop for thirty-one years and says the sales were falling until about four years ago. Then they started rising again, slowly, and mostly to people under thirty.

Nobody in the trade can say exactly why. The explanations come in a predictable order: nostalgia, the cost of a data plan, a backlash against screens. Mink has his own, which he offers only after a pause. "A paper map shows you the whole thing at once," he said. "A phone shows you the next turn. Some people are tired of being told only the next turn."

What the screen hides

A navigation app is built to answer one question: how do I get from here to there in the fastest way. It is very good at that. What it is not built to show is the shape of the land between the two points. A ridge that forces a detour, a river with one bridge, the fact that the quickest road runs along a flood plain. On a sheet of paper these facts sit in plain view, because the designer had no way to hide them.

Cartographers have a name for the trade-off. They call it selection: deciding what to leave out so the important things stand out. A phone screen is a poor place for that kind of judgment, since the screen is small and the software does the choosing without saying so.

“A map asks you to decide where you are. A phone decides it for you, and you never see the decision.”

— Teodor Mink, map seller

Folding it back up

The people who buy paper maps now are not, for the most part, going back to the way things were. Many of them carry a phone as well. They buy the sheet for the planning stage: the evening before a trip, the table after dinner, the moment they want to see how two valleys connect before they commit to a route.

A walker I met near the summit of Ridge Road spread a sheet across a flat rock and traced her finger along a contour line. She had been using the same survey for six years. The creases were soft as cloth. "I know how this hill is shaped now," she said. "I didn't before. My phone told me how long the hill would take."

That is the quiet part of the return. Nobody is rejecting the screen. They are adding a second way of knowing a place, one that is slower to learn and harder to lose.
