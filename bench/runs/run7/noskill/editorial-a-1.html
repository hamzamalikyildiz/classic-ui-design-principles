Clay and Fire

In a converted tannery on the edge of town, one small studio has spent eleven years learning how a pot survives the kiln, and what that survival teaches about patience.

MA

Mara Lindqvist

Contributing writer · 12 min read

Begin with the mud room. Before anything is shaped or glazed, the studio's founder, Ilse Varga, keeps a bucket of wet clay beside the back door and checks it every morning. Too dry, and it cracks in the wheel. Too wet, and it slumps before it can hold a wall. The work starts with a habit of paying attention, long before a pot exists.

The slow middle

Most visitors expect the wheel to be the heart of the place. It is loud, fast and photogenic. Varga is more interested in what happens afterward. A thrown form has to dry evenly, sometimes for a week, before it can be trimmed. Rush it and the base warps. Let it dry too unevenly and it cracks in the bisque, which is the first firing, when the clay turns from fragile to merely brittle.

The studio fires twice a week. Each load is a small negotiation. Shelves are set with posts so no piece touches another, and every glaze is tested on a tile before it reaches a real bowl. A glaze that looks rich on paper can pool into a puddle at 1,280 degrees, or crawl off the foot and leave bare clay exposed.

“A pot doesn't finish when you lift it off the wheel. It finishes when the fire agrees with you.”

Ilse Varga, founder

What the kiln keeps

Ceramics is one of the few crafts where the most important decisions are made after you have stopped working. Glaze chemistry, kiln atmosphere and the speed of cooling all shape the final surface, and the maker can only influence them indirectly. Varga keeps a logbook of every firing, with cone readings, shelf positions and small notes about what went wrong. The logbook is now longer than the studio's price list.

That record has turned failure into an asset. A cracked test tile from 2016 became the basis for the studio's best-selling celadon. A collapsed shelf in 2019 led to a new loading pattern that has cut breakage by more than a third. Nothing is thrown away without first being read.

A room of many hands

The studio employs four people full time and hosts a rotating group of apprentices. Each one spends their first month on the least glamorous task: wedging clay, cutting lids, cleaning the wheels. Only then do they touch a finished piece. The pace is intentionally slow, and Varga admits it does not scale. She has turned down two wholesale contracts to keep it that way.
