Night Train to Trieste

Eleven hours, three borders, and the slow art of arriving somewhere you did not plan to be.

By a contributing writer · 14 min read

The sleeper left Vienna after dark, its corridor lit the color of weak tea. Somewhere past the last suburban lights the conductor slid the door shut, and the train began the long descent toward the Adriatic. Nobody on board had a reason to hurry, and that was the first thing I liked about it.

Trains that run through the night ask you to give up a little control. You cannot see the road, you cannot choose the next stop, and the timetable becomes a rough suggestion. What you get in return is a kind of attention that daytime travel rarely allows. The window becomes a mirror, then a black pane, then suddenly a field full of lamps.

At each border the train stopped and waited. Officials walked the platform with flashlights, passports changed hands, and the carriage settled into a silence that felt almost ceremonial. Each crossing was a small reminder that this route was built out of agreements, many of them older than anyone in the compartment.

“The night is the only part of the journey that cannot be photographed, so you remember it differently.”

From the carriage notebook

By dawn the land had flattened and the air had changed. Karst appeared first as pale stone scattered over hillsides, then as the sea itself, a grey sheet laid out below the tracks. Trieste arrived quietly, with the cranes of the port and the smell of coffee drifting in through a half-open window.

I did not need the destination to justify the trip. The point of a night train is that the journey already has the shape of a story, with a beginning, a long middle in the dark, and an ending that arrives before you have finished thinking about it.
