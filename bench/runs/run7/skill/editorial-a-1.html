Clay and Fire

On a windy bluff above the harbour, four potters have spent eleven years learning how a single kiln load can turn out differently every time.

By Inês Carvalho · Photographs by the studio · 14 min read

The bluff

Vesper Kiln does not advertise. There is a hand-painted sign at the gate and a gravel track that loses most cars before the last bend. Inside the old net shed the benches are scarred with thumbprints and the shelves hold bowls that have not yet been sold, each one labelled in pencil with the date of its firing.

Ruth Halloran founded the studio after leaving a commercial tableware factory in the city. She wanted the work to be slower and less certain. Her first kiln was a second-hand brick box that she rebuilt twice before it held a steady temperature. “The first year I was measuring everything,” she says. “The second year I started listening to the fire.”

The four potters who now share the bluff each keep a separate wheel and a separate shelf, but they fire together. A single load can hold forty pieces from five different hands, and the kiln does not care whose name is on the base.

“Clay remembers every decision you made about it, and the fire writes the final sentence.”

Ruth Halloran, co-founder

Nine hours at the mouth

A firing begins before sunrise with the slow climb. For the first three hours the burners are barely lit and the kiln is allowed to dry out the last of its moisture. Anything rushed here cracks later. Tomas Reyes, who runs the north kiln, keeps a logbook with the temperature every fifteen minutes and a note on the wind.

Cone six, the target for most of the stoneware, is not a number on a dial. It is a small ceramic pyramid that bends when the clay has reached maturity. The team watches it through a peephole and calls the shut-down when it has bowed to the shelf. Nobody argues with the cone.

Afterwards, the cooling takes another day. Opening early exposes the pots to cold air and they can craze or split. So the studio waits, and in that waiting the makers sit on the step with tea, looking at the sea and talking about the pieces they hope to see when the door finally opens.

Glaze

Ruth Halloran

Runs the glaze books and the seasonal test tiles. Her celadons come from ash collected off the ridge.

Kiln

Tomas Reyes

Keeps the north kiln running and the logbook honest. Fires the big planters that take a full day to cool.

Form

Mariam Okafor

Throws the tall bottles and the thin-walled cups. Her wheel faces the window so she can watch the weather.

Surface

Jun Mata

Carves, stamps and inlays with iron oxide. The marks on the base of every piece are his.
