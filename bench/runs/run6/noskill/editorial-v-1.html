Field Notes Essay No. 12

Cartography & Power

The Quiet Power of Maps

Every map is an argument about what matters, what can be left out, and who gets to stand at the center.

By the Editors/9 min read

A map looks like a window. It seems to show the world as it is, neutral and measured, drawn to scale by people who had no particular stake in the outcome. That appearance is the map's most powerful trick. Every line on a sheet of paper or a glowing screen is the result of a decision, and most of those decisions were made long before anyone opened the file.

Choosing what to leave out

Consider the simplest question a cartographer faces: what goes on the page? A road network can be drawn as a dense tangle of every lane, or as a few confident arteries. A river can be a thread or a ribbon. Each choice reduces the planet to something a human eye can read, and each reduction hides a great deal. The map you hold is a summary written in a language of omission.

That omission is not inherently sinister. A transit map that drew every street would be useless to a commuter. But the same logic, applied carelessly or deliberately, can erase a neighbourhood, shrink a border, or make a remote settlement disappear altogether.

“The projection you choose decides which countries look large and which look small, long before anyone asks what they are worth.”

The problem of the flat world

Flattening a sphere onto a rectangle requires distortion. There is no escape from this geometry. A projection that keeps shapes accurate will stretch distances; one that preserves areas will skew angles. Mercator, the sixteenth-century projection still common on wall charts and web tiles, keeps directions true for navigation and inflates the high latitudes until they seem to rival continents. The distortion was a feature for sailors. For everyone else it quietly rearranged the sense of how big the world is.

There is no innocent projection. There are only projections whose distortions we have learned to stop noticing.

Who gets the center

Maps have a center, and the center is rarely a neutral point. Many world maps place the prime meridian and the equator at the heart of the sheet, which puts a particular set of nations at the middle of the story and pushes others to the edges. Reversing the orientation, or centering on the Pacific, is an act that takes a moment to feel strange. That strangeness is information about how much of the world's self-image is built into the grid.

Reading a map against the grain

The practical lesson is not to distrust maps wholesale. It is to read them the way one reads any careful argument: ask what is being emphasised, what has been reduced, and what the scale is quietly assuming. Check the projection in the small print. Notice which countries are drawn large. Look for what is missing before admiring what is shown.

Good cartographers have always known this. The best maps of the last century often announce their own limits, with dashed borders for contested land, insets that correct scale, and legends that explain their symbols. They treat the reader as a participant rather than a recipient.

A window with a frame

The map will keep its authority, and it should. Navigation, disaster response and urban planning all depend on it. But authority is not the same as truth. A map is a frame placed around a view, and the frame is always someone's hand. Once you can see the frame, the view becomes far more interesting.
