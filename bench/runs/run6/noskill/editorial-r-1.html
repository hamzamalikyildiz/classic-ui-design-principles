The Quiet Return of the Paper Map

Phones promised to make folding a map obsolete. Instead, a generation that never learned to read one is buying them by the armful, and the reasons say a lot about how we want to be lost.

MR

By Maren Ruiz

Photographs by Idris Okafor · 14 min read

Ask a cartographer when the paper map stopped mattering and you will get a shrug and a date: 2011, or possibly 2014, the year navigation moved into every pocket and the glovebox atlas became a coaster. The story was tidy and, for a while, true. Nobody needed to know where the river bent when a blue dot could tell them.

Yet the maps did not disappear. Bookshops in several cities report that atlases now sell faster than cookbooks, and outdoor retailers have quietly widened their shelves of sheet maps, waterproof and tear-resistant, printed on paper that feels better in the hand than it has any right to.

Part of this is nostalgia, and nostalgia is not a small force. But the people I spoke with were rarely nostalgic about the maps themselves. They talked about attention. A paper sheet shows the whole city at once, so you notice that the park you were heading for sits beside a street you had never heard of. A screen shows only the next turn.

"You stop following a line and start making decisions. The map asks you to be a little bit lost on purpose."

— Sunniva Halvorsen, cartographer

Halvorsen, who draws trail maps for a small press in the north of Norway, says the difference is mostly about memory. People who navigate with a blue dot remember the route as a sequence of commands. Those who unfold a sheet remember it as a place. Her studies, small and informal as she admits they are, point the same way.

There is also the matter of trust. A digital map is revised silently and continuously, which is a kindness until a road closes and nobody tells you. A printed sheet carries its date on its face. You know exactly how old it is, and that honesty has become a selling point in its own right.

None of this means the paper map is coming back to replace the phone. The more likely outcome is a quieter division of labour: the screen for getting somewhere in a hurry, the sheet for deciding where to go at all.
