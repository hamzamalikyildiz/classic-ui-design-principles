The Quiet Return of the Paper Map

Phone navigation told us where we were. A folded sheet, bought at a petrol station and spread across a car bonnet, is teaching a new generation to ask where they are going.

By Maren Oduya · 2,400 words · 11 min read

Nobody I asked could remember the last time they unfolded a map in a car. The memory came back slowly, in pieces: a glovebox that never quite closed, a crease that had worn through at the centre, a finger tracing a blue line toward a town nobody in the car had visited.

Paper maps did not disappear because they stopped working. They disappeared because a blue dot did the job more quickly, and because the dot never asked anything of us. It rerouted, it apologised, and it kept the big picture to itself.

What the sheet gives back

A paper sheet shows the whole district at once. You see the ridge before you see the turning, and the river you would have crossed without noticing. Planning a route on one is slower, and that slowness is the point: you are forced to decide what matters before you set off.

Ruth Halloran, who has run a walking-map shop in the Peak District for nineteen years, says her customers now buy the sheets for the wall first. “They want to know where the hills are before they want to know where to park,” she said, pulling a 1:25,000 sheet from a drawer.

“A map asks you to be a little lost first, and then to decide where you are.”

Ruth Halloran, cartographer

Folding is a skill

The hardest part of a paper map is refolding it. Most people fold along the wrong lines, end up with a brick of paper, and give up. The sheets sold in the new shops are printed with the fold pattern in faint grey, a small courtesy that tells you the designers expected you to struggle.

There is also a kind of attention that only paper allows. You hold the scale in your head, you count the contours by eye, and you notice that the church at the top of the valley is marked twice because the surveyors were working from different years.

None of this makes the paper map better than the screen. It makes it different, and the difference is a reason to choose it on a Sunday afternoon with nowhere in particular to be.
