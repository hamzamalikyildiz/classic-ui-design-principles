The Map Is Not the Territory, and Neither Is the Screen in Your Hand

Every map is a decision about what to leave out. The history of cartography is a history of those decisions, and of the people who were quietly wrong about them.

By Elena Varga · 11 min read

Ask most people where Greenland sits relative to Africa and they will point to something close to equal size. On the common world projection, Greenland looks roughly the size of the entire continent. In reality Africa is about fourteen times larger. The error is not a mistake of arithmetic. It is a consequence of trying to flatten a sphere onto a rectangle, and it has shaped how generations of readers understood their place in the world.

A Chart Built for Sailors

The medieval portolan charts that guided Mediterranean sailors were not built on any projection at all. They were webs of rhumb lines radiating from compass roses, good for plotting a course by ruler and dividers and nothing more. Their coastlines were remarkably accurate, because the people who used them would drown if they were not.

Ptolemy, writing centuries earlier, had tried something grander: a grid of latitude and longitude across the known inhabited world. His coordinates were often wrong, and his maps inherited the mistakes of their sources, but the idea that a map could be a mathematical object rather than a sketch was the seed of everything that followed.

A map is an argument about the world, presented as if it were a window onto it.

Editorial note on the essay's central claim

The Projection Problem

In 1569 Gerardus Mercator published a world map that solved a practical problem for navigators: a straight line on the chart was a course of constant compass bearing. The trade-off was that land near the poles swelled dramatically. The map was superb for plotting a voyage and poor at telling you how big anything was.

Every projection is a compromise. Some preserve area, some preserve shape, some keep distances honest along a few chosen routes. None preserves everything, which is why a reasonable cartographer picks a projection for a purpose and says so on the page.

The screen in your pocket has made the choice invisible. The default web map is a single projection, rendered at every zoom level, and most people never see the seam. That invisibility is the real danger: a choice that no longer looks like a choice.
