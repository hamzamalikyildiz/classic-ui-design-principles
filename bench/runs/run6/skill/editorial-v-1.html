The Map Is Never the Territory, and Never Quite Neutral

Every coastline we trust was drawn by someone who wanted a particular route to look inevitable.

By Miriam Okafor · 9 min read

Open any atlas and the coastline looks like a fact. It has the calm authority of ink on good paper, and it asks nothing of the reader. Yet every line on that page is a decision: what to include, what to leave blank, which river gets a name and which marsh is simply erased.

Early surveyors worked with rope, compass bearings and the patience of people who walked the same ground twice. Their results were rough, and they knew it. The pressure to produce a clean sheet came later, from patrons who wanted routes to look inevitable and harbours to look ready for trade.

The result is a document that performs certainty. A dashed line across a blank sheet implies that the way is clear. A river drawn with a steady hand implies it can be forded. Neither claim may be true, and neither is marked as a guess.

“A map tells you where someone once wanted you to go, which is useful, but not the same as where the land will let you walk.”

— From the survey logbook, 1794

Reading the blank spaces

Historians of cartography have learned to read the blanks as carefully as the lines. A margin left empty can mean that no one went there, that the surveyor was refused entry, or that the information was too dangerous to publish. Each reason tells a different story about power.

Modern digital maps hide their blanks well. Tiles load smoothly, labels resolve on demand, and the gaps are filled with plausible grey. The interface feels complete, which is exactly why it deserves the same questions we once asked of a hand-drawn sheet.

The practical habit is simple. Before trusting a route, ask what the map was made to show, who paid for it, and what it leaves out on purpose. A good navigator keeps the paper map in the pack and checks the ground.
