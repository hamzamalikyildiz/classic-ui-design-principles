Spruce and Varnish

Inside the Cremona-trained workshop of Matteo Bellini, where 18th-century instruments are stabilized with hide glue, bone dust, and patience.

By Clara Lindqvist · October 14, 2026 · 11 min read

At seven in the morning, before the tram rattles past Via Beltrami, Matteo Bellini boils a small copper pot of rabbit-skin glue. The scent is faintly sweet and unmistakably organic—an adhesive that has held European string instruments together for four centuries because it can be undone with a damp cloth and warm steam.

On his bench rests a 1742 Venetian violin whose bass bar has begun to fatigue under three hundred years of string tension. A modern epoxy would silence the top plate forever. Bellini’s work is the opposite of permanence: every repair must be reversible by a restorer who has not yet been born.

Listening to the Wood

Before touching a chisel, Bellini taps the spruce belly with the knuckle of his middle finger, holding the instrument at its nodal line. He is listening for the tap tone—a clear, woody F-sharp that tells him whether a hairline crack along the f-hole is merely cosmetic or structurally loose.

“A violin is a lung,” he says, setting the instrument on a padded leather cradle. “If you stiffen the rib or over-patch the spruce, it still looks like a Stradivarius in a glass case, but it stops breathing under the bow.”

The Alchemy of Retouching

The hardest hour of restoration is not structural; it is chromatic. When a missing sliver of maple is grafted into a worn scroll, the fresh wood gleams pale against centuries of amber oil varnish. Bellini grinds madder lake, alizarin, and walnut husk by hand, building translucent glazes in twelve-hour intervals until the patch disappears under northern skylight.

By five o’clock, the Venetian violin is strung again with gut-core A and D strings. Bellini draws a single slow open fifth. The room fills with a dry, resonant warmth—older than any living person in it, and ready for another century.
