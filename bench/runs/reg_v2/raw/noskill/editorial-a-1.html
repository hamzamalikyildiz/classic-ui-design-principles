Spruce and Varnish

Inside the north-facing Cremona workshop where Matteo Bellini spends months coaxing three-hundred-year-old instruments back into voice.

By Clara Vance · 12 Min Read · Autumn 2026

At seven in the morning, before the delivery vans rattle over the cobblestones of Via Beltrami, Matteo Bellini opens the tall shutters of his workshop. North light—even, cool, and unforgiving—spills across a workbench scarred by forty years of chisels, hide-glue pots, and brass planes no larger than a walnut. On a linen pad sits a 1728 violin whose spruce top has parted along a centuries-old winter grain.

Restoration, Bellini insists, is not the art of making an old thing look new. It is the discipline of listening to what wood remembers. Every violin carries the climate of its birth: the tight, slow-growing rings of high-altitude Paneveggio spruce, the mineral trace of the river water used to cook the animal glue, and the subtle compression left by generations of chinrests and bow pressure.

The Patience of Hide Glue

Modern synthetics are banned from this room. To close a crack in a soundboard, Bellini warms pearl-grade hide glue in a copper bain-marie until it reaches sixty degrees Celsius—hot enough to flow into microscopic fibers, cool enough not to scorch the collagen. If a future restorer needs to open the instrument fifty years from now, a dampened palette knife will release the seam without tearing a single cell of spruce.

“You never force wood to meet your hand. You humidify, you wait, and you let the arch remember where it belongs.”

— Matteo Bellini, Cremona

Once the structural seam is stable, attention turns to the varnish. Original eighteenth-century oil varnishes—amber resin, linseed oil, and madder lake—have a depth that refracts light like honey over dark stone. Retouching a worn corner requires layering spirit-based pigments with a squirrel-hair brush, building transparency one breath-thin coat at a time until the repair disappears into the patina.

When a soloist finally draws a bow across the restored strings, Bellini does not watch the player’s hands. He watches the windowpane, listening for the quiet bloom of overtones that tells him the spruce is breathing freely again.
