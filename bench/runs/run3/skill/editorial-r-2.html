The Quiet Return of the Paper Map

Phones promised to make folded paper obsolete. Hikers, city planners and a surprising number of designers have quietly kept it in their pockets.

Owen Marlowe

Staff writer · 12 min read · Photographs by the author

Every few years someone declares the paper map dead, and every few years the declaration gets quietly revised. This autumn, the people who sell and print maps report steady orders, and walk into any outdoor shop and you will see racks of folded sheets beside the GPS watches.

The reasons are practical before they are nostalgic. A paper sheet does not run out of battery on a ridge, does not drop signal in a canyon and does not decide, at an unmarked junction, that the road you want is a footpath.

“A map asks you to decide where you are. A phone decides for you.”

Cartographers say the appeal is also about attention. A paper sheet shows the whole valley at once, so the eye wanders and finds the stream you would have missed on a screen that scrolls one blue dot at a time.

Why the fold matters

Designers who work with place names say the physical object carries a kind of memory. You learn its creases, you know which corner is the north edge, and you remember where you were when a line first made sense to you.

None of this means the phone is going away. Most walkers still carry both. The difference is that the paper is no longer the thing you apologise for bringing.

By the numbers

41%

of surveyed hikers said they carry a paper map on day walks, up from 27 percent in 2021.
