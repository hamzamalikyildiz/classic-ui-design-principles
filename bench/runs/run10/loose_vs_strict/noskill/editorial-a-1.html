The Last Lighthouse Keepers

For more than a century, a person climbed the stairs every night to trim the wick. Automation arrived quietly, and the keepers who were left had to decide what a light is for.

By Eleanor Marsh / Photographs and reporting from the west coast / 2 November 2026

The light at Ardnish Point was lit by hand for one hundred and twelve years. Each dusk the keeper walked the iron stairs, checked the lamp and the clockwork that turned the lens, and wrote the weather in a ledger that the Commissioners would later read. The work was dull for most of every night and urgent for about ten minutes at a time, when fog came in off the water.

Automation did not arrive with a ceremony. A sensor on the gallery rail began to switch the lamp on when the light failed, and a radio link reported the state of the lamp to a control room some two hundred miles inland. For a while the keepers stayed on, doing maintenance and keeping the ledgers. Then the ledgers stopped being needed, and the houses were offered for sale.

“You stop being the person who keeps the light and become the person who notices when it stops.”

— Iain Carrick, keeper at Ardnish, 1978–2003

Keepers describe the change in terms of attention. A flame that needs watching keeps a person awake in a particular way. A sensor that reports its own failure does not need the same kind of watching, and the job that was once continuous became a set of checks made at intervals by people many miles away. Most of the men and women who held the post for decades say they miss the nights more than the wages.

Heritage bodies now keep a handful of the old stations working as tourist sites and as back-up beacons. The lenses are polished by volunteers. The ledgers, bound in green cloth, are kept in a reading room on the mainland, where visitors can turn the pages and see the weather of a single night written in a neat hand.

A short timeline of automation

  1. 1986 Remote monitoring trials begin at three stations on the north coast.
  2. 1998 Automatic lamp changeover replaces the keeper on night watch at most sites.
  3. 2003 Ardnish Point becomes unmanned, and the final keeper leaves the house.
  4. 2019 The last staffed light in the region is switched to remote control.
