The Last Lighthouse Keepers

For two centuries a light was kept by people who lived beside it. Now most of them are gone, and the job is done by clocks, sensors and a few visits a year.

By Mara Holloway · November 2026 · 12 min read

Every night at dusk, somewhere on a rocky shore, a lamp is switched on and a lens turns. For most of recorded history someone had to make that happen. A keeper climbed the stairs with a can of paraffin, trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork that spun the lens and wrote the log by hand before dawn.

The keepers did more than light the lamp. They watched the weather, recorded the barometer, fired signals in fog and kept the brass polished so that the light would be seen clearly from a ship. On many stations the whole family lived in the tower or in cottages at its foot, and a posting could last a lifetime.

“You did not own the light. You simply made sure it never went out on your watch.”

A retired principal keeper, speaking about his final years on the rock

How the work left the rock

Automation did not arrive in one stroke. Sealed lamps, electric power and timers came first, then remote monitoring that let a shore station see a fault before a ship did. Each change looked small on paper. Together they removed the reason a family had to stay.

The savings were real, and so were the costs. Keepers knew the sound a failing bulb made and the smell of a generator about to quit. Those habits went quietly out of the service with them, and few of the replacement systems were built to be listened to.

A short timeline

  1. 1810

    The Bell Rock light, built on a reef off Scotland, is lit and staffed by keepers living on the rock.

  2. 1990

    Keeper numbers fall across the major services as remote monitoring spreads to coastal stations.

  3. 1998

    The last manned stations in the service are switched to automatic operation, and the keepers’ cottages are sold or left empty.

What is still climbed

Some towers still need a person. Lenses are cleaned, batteries replaced and salt scrubbed from glass that no machine can reach. A visiting crew arrives by boat, works through the day and leaves before the tide turns. Their log is shorter than a keeper’s, but the stairs are the same.
