The Last Lighthouse Keepers

Across the northern coasts, the automatic beacon has replaced the people who once climbed the tower every night. Some keepers stayed on anyway.

By Elif Arslan · 12 min read

Illustration: a single beam over the last working station on the northern headland.

For forty years the rhythm of the headland was set by a man with a logbook. He wound the clockwork, trimmed the wick, and wrote the weather down by hand every four hours, whatever the sea was doing.

Now the light turns by itself. A sensor on the mast reads the dusk, a relay clicks, and a lamp that once needed oil and attention runs for months without anyone setting foot inside. The coastal authority calls this a success. The shipping log calls it quiet.

“The light never needed me. The people who passed the station did.”

Former keeper, interviewed in the station kitchen

Not every station has gone dark. On three rocky points, a handful of keepers remain on paid contracts to watch the instruments, climb the stairs when the fog bell fails, and answer calls from fishing boats that still prefer a human voice on the radio.

Their days are less about the lamp and more about the coast around it: clearing gull nests from the gallery rail, logging erosion on the path, and keeping a patient eye on the weather that the automated systems describe in numbers but cannot quite feel.

A short timeline of automation

  1. 1950s · Clockwork and oil lamps give way to electric lamps on the main headlands.
  2. 1980s · Remote monitoring links the stations to a central office on the mainland.
  3. 2002 · The first fully automated station is switched on without a resident keeper.
  4. 2015 · The last staffed station on the eastern shelf is de-manned.
  5. Today · Three staffed points remain, on contracts renewed each year.

The question is no longer whether the lights will run. It is who will notice when something in the dark goes wrong, and whether a sensor can be trusted to say so.
