Spruce and Varnish

Inside Matteo Bellini’s quiet Milanese workshop, where three-hundred-year-old instruments are coaxed back to voice with hide glue, bone dust, and patience.

By Elena Marchetti · October 14, 2026 · 9 min read

Every morning at seven, before the trams begin rattling down Via Solferino, Matteo Bellini opens the tall wooden shutters of his third-floor studio and checks the hygrometer on the north wall. Forty-eight percent relative humidity. Anything higher, and the Alpine spruce top of a 1715 Cremonese violin will swell by fractions of a millimeter; anything lower, and an old seam along the bass bar might whisper open overnight.

Bellini has spent thirty-four years repairing instruments that have outlived empires. On his bench today rests a Venetian cello with a hairline soundpost crack—the most delicate fracture in string restoration. Unlike modern woodworking, where strength is the sole metric, a restorer’s repair must remain invisible, reversible, and acoustically transparent.

The Ethics of the Invisible Seam

To close a crack in aged spruce, Bellini never reaches for synthetic resin. Instead, he warms a small pot of rabbit-skin hide glue in a water bath until it reaches sixty degrees Celsius. The glue’s protein chains bond with the wood fibers yet can be softened decades later with a damp cloth and gentle heat, allowing future luthiers to undo his work without scarring the original plate.

Once the crack is aligned using custom-carved counter-forms cast in plaster, he reinforces the underside with tiny diamond-shaped spruce cleats, each planed to a thickness of less than a millimeter and oriented so their grain crosses the fracture at a slight angle.

Amber, Oil, and Patience

Varnish work is the final test of restraint. A retouched patch on a worn maple rib cannot look freshly lacquered; it must match the oxidized patina of linseed oil, Baltic amber, and Venetian turpentine laid down three centuries ago. Bellini mixes dry mineral pigments on a glass slab with a palette knife, testing each tint under north daylight before touching a sable brush to the wood.

By late afternoon, the cello’s seam has disappeared to the naked eye. Bellini sets the instrument back in its velvet-lined cradle, wipes his chisels with camellia oil, and lets the glue cure until morning.
